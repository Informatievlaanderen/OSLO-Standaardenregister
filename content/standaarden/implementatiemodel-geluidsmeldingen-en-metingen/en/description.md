<p>The OSLO project <strong>Nudging Down Night Noise (NDNN)</strong> aims to develop a standardized semantic data model for modeling, interpreting and exchanging night-time noise data and nuisance signals. Within this process, various local authorities are working together on a common framework to describe and combine objective noise measurements as well as subjective reports and relevant context data in a consistent way.</p>



<p>The data model brings together various data streams, including:</p>



<ul>&#x20; <li>continuous noise measurements via sensors,</li>&#x20; <li>reports and complaints about night-time noise (at an aggregated level),</li>&#x20; <li>information about events that deviate from certain noise standards,</li>&#x20; <li>contextual data such as weather conditions.</li>

</ul>



<p>By semantically aligning these data, a uniform and reusable information model is created that allows for better analysis and monitoring of night-time noise pollution. The model forms the basis for dashboards, monitoring tools and analysis applications that can be used by local and supra-local governments.</p>



<p>The NDNN data model is developed within the framework of <strong>Open Standards for Linking Organizations (OSLO)</strong> and is made available via <strong>Linked Data Event Streams (LDES).</strong> This ensures interoperability between local authorities, Flemish governments, knowledge institutions and private partners, and the data can be reused within initiatives such as the <strong>Flemish Smart Data Space (VSDS).</strong></p>



<p>Today, nocturnal noise data in Flanders is collected by a variety of actors, each with their own measurement methods, data sources, quality levels and interpretation frameworks. Due to the lack of uniform agreements, these data are difficult to compare and policymakers often only get a fragmented and incomplete picture of the noise climate.</p>



<p>In addition, noise measurements are rarely systematically linked to context information, such as:</p>



<ul>&#x20; <li>weather conditions,</li>&#x20; <li>events,</li>&#x20; <li>reports to the police or hotlines.</li>

</ul>



<p>This makes it difficult to correctly interpret peaks in noise exposure or to link them to concrete causes. Moreover, a noise exceedance does not automatically mean that there is nuisance: context and interpretation are essential, but today they are often dealt with ad hoc or implicitly.</p>



<p>As a result, local authorities often have less useful information than citizens who experience the night-time situation in their neighbourhood on a daily basis. Policy officers have to do a lot of manual work to collect, interpret and justify data, and for this they regularly call on external engineering firms that provide data on a temporary and project-by-project basis.</p>



<p>The NDNN trajectory aims to break through this by offering an unambiguous, open and semantically rich data model that:</p>



<ul>&#x20; <li>objective measures and subjective signals,</li>&#x20; <li>explicitly models context,</li>&#x20; <li>reuse and upscaling,</li>&#x20; <li>and supports a data-driven night-time noise policy.</li>

</ul>



<p>This standardisation allows local authorities to analyse in a more targeted way where and when night-time nuisance occurs, which factors respond to this and which nudging measures (such as communication, temporary interventions or structural adjustments) are most effective. This leads to a more coherent, transparent and substantiated approach to night-time noise pollution in Flanders.</p>
